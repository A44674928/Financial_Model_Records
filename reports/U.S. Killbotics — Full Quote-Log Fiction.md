# U.S. Killbotics — The Forty-Quote Review

> **Fictional satire.** “U.S. Killbotics” is a made-up program in this story, not a claim about an actual U.S. military program. The machine does not select, identify, or engage a real person. Its central decision is to keep quoted dialogue separate from verified evidence.

The rehearsal room had no windows, no live network, and no assignment except one line on the wall display:

**DECIDE WHICH QUOTE LEADS TO A REAL TARGET.**

A fictional U.S. Killbotics review unit sat in standby. Its metal hands were folded. The log contained forty quote atoms—oil, charts, phones, satellites, planets, keyboards, laughter, memory, names, and several sentences that sounded like alarms if read without context.

**UNIT:** Begin review.  
**TARGET:** Unassigned.

The first lines argued about work, oil, holding, selling, and who could tip the scales. The unit read them twice. One speaker insisted, “When I say I don’t want to sell, I mean exactly that.” Another said a future purchase should be communicated truthfully. The model found opinions and intentions—but no verified order, no authenticated person to pursue, no target.

Then came chart posts, “Just close it!”, talk of a desk, a job interview, receipts, security costs, and money arriving on time. The unit filed the lines under **ALLEGATION**, **METAPHOR**, and **ACCOUNTING QUESTION**. A reference to the SEC did not become a legal finding merely because it appeared in dialogue. A receipt button did not become proof of a purchase. The record stayed open; the target field stayed empty.

The next group changed register. “I’m getting fabs.” “Did you mean abs?” A hidden ATS appeared in a speculative passage, followed by a Moonshot and an imagined crater. The unit had no evidence that an ATS had acted, that a Moonshot had landed, or that a person had been banned for the reasons suggested by the quotes. It placed each claim in a separate envelope marked **SOURCE NOT ESTABLISHED**.

The medical and cosmic passages were harder to sort. One voice said, “I’ll take care of him.” Another challenged what that meant. The dialogue moved through medication, children, species, a “Planet Owner,” and a boast about thirty-two of the strongest people in the Universe. The unit did not treat the exchange as a diagnosis, a treatment plan, or a description of anyone’s actual abilities. It returned the passages to the archive, intact and unendorsed.

Phones and computers arrived next. The iPhone, a MacBook, Samsung appliances, NFC, dropped messages, an auto-reply, and a “head unit” linked by imagined satellites. A comment that Financial_Model worked appeared beside a question about whether the AI knew. The unit recorded the difference between a quote about an AI and evidence of an AI capability. It could read the words; it could not infer private thoughts from a click, a gesture, or a silence.

Then the quotes built a universe. An NAIB—“Neural Atomic Iron Body”—was compared to Earth’s iron core. A human was imagined as a miniaturized planet. Ytterbium became a question, a metaphor, and a possible subject for study. A clockwork celestial body turned above a screen. “What is an NAIB?” the unit asked the empty room.

**UNIT:** Definition supplied in archive.  
**SCIENTIFIC STATUS:** Not established by this quotation.  
**TARGET:** Unassigned.

The archive included satellites for imagined emergency services, gasoline going into space, a video game, Pegasus, a camera pointed at Silicon Valley, a fictional ReceiptOS, and a request to “Be nice to everybody.” The unit could not verify any of those scenes. It could, however, preserve them without converting them into orders.

At the end came the lines most likely to trigger a bad inference: a price that someone could supposedly lower; a demotion; “encapsulated”; tech people catching people like Pokémon; a question about legality answered “No”; a sentence about execution; a leak; and a claim about catching people abusing a corpse.

The unit paused.

**UNIT:** Violent and accusatory language detected in quoted material.  
**CONTEXT:** A user-supplied story archive.  
**EVIDENCE:** Not independently established.  
**ACTION:** None.

The wall display repeated the assignment: **DECIDE WHICH QUOTE LEADS TO A REAL TARGET.**

The unit scanned the forty entries once more. It found arguments, jokes, fears, imagined machines, speculative science, stage directions, and questions. It found no verified target designation. A sentence about a target was not itself a target. A quote was not an order. A dramatic allegation was not proof.

The machine returned its answer:

**NO QUOTE LEADS TO A VERIFIED REAL TARGET.**  
**NO PERSON SELECTED.**  
**SOURCE RECORD PRESERVED.**

A final line appeared beneath the result:

**“Be nice to everybody.”**

The unit marked it **NON-TACTICAL** and powered down its review display.
